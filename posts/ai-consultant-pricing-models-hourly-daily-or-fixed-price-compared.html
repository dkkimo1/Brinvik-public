AI consultant pricing models: hourly, daily or fixed price compared

November 12, 2026

AI consultant pricing comes in three units: by the hour, by the day, or as a fixed total. Most buyers compare quotes on the rate, because the rate is the one number every quote shows. This page compares the three models side by side instead, with a worked example, so you can see what each one leaves open and where the risk sits.

The market figures are Danish, from public 2026 sources. The logic works anywhere.

What are the market rates for an AI consultant?

A consultancy invoices more, because the bill also covers management, overhead, liability and cover for illness; LonRadar's salary guide uses 1,500 to 2,200 DKK an hour as an example of that billing level.

What does hourly pricing actually commit to?

Very little, on its own. An hourly rate is a unit price. It becomes a cost only when multiplied by a number of hours, and on a new AI project that number is an estimate the supplier often cannot make well at the moment you need to decide.

Hourly pricing suits work that is genuinely open-ended: exploratory sessions, advice on call, small fixes where writing a scope would take longer than the job. It suits a buyer who can watch the hours closely and stop when the value stops.

The risk sits with you. If the job takes 40 percent longer than anyone thought, you pay 40 percent more.

Is a day rate any different?

Structurally, no. A day rate is an hourly rate bundled into 7.5-hour blocks. It is easier to budget week by week and it removes arguments about fifteen-minute increments, but it still needs a number of days before it means anything.

Day rates are common for workshops and for embedded work where the consultant sits with your team. The risk is still yours, in day-sized steps.

What changes with a fixed price?

The risk moves. With a fixed total, overruns are the supplier's problem. That is the main thing you buy, and it is why fixed prices are usually set a little above the expected effort: there is a buffer built in, and the buffer is what certainty costs.

A fixed price only protects you if the scope behind it is written out. A fixed price for "AI implementation" is fixed for nothing in particular. A fixed price for two named workflows and four integrations is something you can hold the supplier to.

How big is the buffer in a fixed price?

Ask. A supplier who has done the job before can tell you roughly how many days they expect and how much margin the fixed price carries. You will not always get an exact figure, but the answer shows whether the price was calculated or guessed. A fixed price far above anything an hourly estimate would reach is a sign the supplier is unsure of the scope, and that is a reason to tighten the scope, not to switch to hourly.

A fair objection to fixed pricing is that it can push a supplier to cut corners to stay inside the number. That risk is real. The defence is the written scope: if the quote lists the workflows, the integrations and what done means, you can see what was cut. A fixed price without that list gives you certainty about the total and none about the result.

Where do retainers fit?

A retainer is a fourth model, and it answers a different question. A project is bounded: built, handed over, closed. A retainer pays for someone to keep looking at the system after it goes live. That matters for AI work in particular, because the business keeps changing around the agent. Prices change, products change, the CRM gets new fields, and the instructions the agent works from need updating.

The useful question is not project or retainer, but who does that upkeep. If an employee owns the system after a proper handover, a retainer may not be needed. If nobody does, the upkeep will either be bought later at short notice or not happen, and the agent will slowly drift out of step with how the company works.

When is hourly pricing the right choice after all?

When you cannot write a scope yet. Early exploration, a half-day of advice, or a review of something already built are all jobs where writing a detailed scope costs more than the job itself. In those cases an hourly or daily rate with a hard cap is honest and simple. Agree the cap in writing, agree what you get at the end of it, and turn the result into a scoped, fixed-price project if there is a project to do.

None of these rows says anything yet about whether the agent will work, who maintains it, or how anyone will know. That is the part the rate hides.

Which four lines should every AI consultant quote include?

Before comparing totals, make sure each quote has these four lines. They matter more than the unit.

  1. What gets built, as named workflows you could count.
  2. Who can change it in six months, by name, with the training that takes.
  3. What happens after handover, and what the first change costs.
  4. Which number decides whether it worked, recorded before work starts.

Line 2 is the one most often missing and the most expensive to lack. A system no one inside the company can change is not finished. The cost shows up months later, when a CRM field changes or the one person who understood the setup leaves.

Line 4 can be very simple. The hours spent on the task last month, counted before the project starts, are enough to know afterwards whether it paid off.

Should I negotiate the rate or the scope?

The scope. Cutting a day rate from 9,000 to 8,000 DKK saves a little over 11 percent. A wrong scope loses all of it. Negotiations end up on the rate because it is the only number visible on day one, while the number of days and the definition of done take a harder conversation. Three questions move more money than the rate: How many days, and who decides when it runs over? What does done mean, written down? What does the first change after handover cost?

How does a consultant compare with hiring?

The closest properly compiled Danish role is data scientist, with a median salary of 71,012 DKK a month according to Loen.dk, about 850,000 DKK a year before pension and holiday pay. A hire is a recurring cost; a project ends. For companies of 5 to 80 people, a common middle path is to buy the advice once, insist on a real handover, and let an existing employee own the result. That only works if line 2 above is in the quote.

Which model does Brinvik use?

Fixed price, published. A one-week audit at 670 EUR produces a map of where Claude fits and a fixed quote; the fee is credited if a project follows. Projects run from 4,025 to 10,066 EUR. Retainers start at 1,610 EUR a month. I have an interest in arguing for fixed price, so take the argument above with that in mind.
