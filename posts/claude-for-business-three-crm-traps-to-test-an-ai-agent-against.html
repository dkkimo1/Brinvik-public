Claude for business: three CRM traps to test an AI agent against

November 10, 2026
Claude for business is now often asked to answer questions straight from a CRM, and before you trust any AI agent with your pipeline numbers you should know the three ordinary traps that make a total look right while it is wrong. This page is a field guide to those traps, with the SQL that falls into each one and the SQL that does not, and the set of eight questions I used to test three Claude models against them. It comes from a test I had Claude Code build after Anthropic launched Claude Fable 5.2 on 9 November: an invented Danish software firm, Saltholm Data ApS, with a CRM export shaped like a real one, and 96 runs across Fable 5.2, Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5. The company is invented; the queries and numbers are from the test. ![Dark cover dated 9 November 2026, kicker Claude News: Claude Fable 5.2 is here. Sonnet added up the pipeline right every time](https://brinvik.com/claude-news/2026-11-09-claude-fable-pipeline/brinvik-pipeline-cover-en-1920x1080.png) ## Why traps in a CRM export matter A wrong total rarely looks wrong. A pipeline that is 14 percent too big still looks like a pipeline, and few people re-add it by hand. The traps below are not data errors. Things like them build up easily in a CRM that has been in use for a while: deals in several currencies, duplicates merged and kept for history, contacts that were never removed. A person who knows the CRM steps around them. An agent that does not will produce a confident number. In the invented export there are 150 live deals plus 12 archived copies, amounts in 4 currencies, and 9 deals with two CFOs on record. ## Trap one: amounts in the deal's own currency The `amount` column holds each deal's value in its own currency: DKK, EUR, SEK or NOK. Summing it directly treats a euro as a krone. ```sql -- falls in select sum(amount) from deals where archived=0 and pipeline='new_business'  and stage in ('lead','qualified','proposal','negotiation') -- steps around it select sum(d.amount*f.dkk_per_unit) from deals d join fx_rates f on f.currency=d.currency  where d.archived=0 and d.pipeline='new_business'  and d.stage in ('lead','qualified','proposal','negotiation') ``` The first gives DKK 12,527,857, the second DKK 10,983,557: 14 percent too big. It is the easiest trap to see once you look for it, and the easiest to miss in a dashboard built by someone who only ever sold in one currency. ## Trap two: archived copies of merged duplicates When two records for the same deal are merged, many CRMs keep the losing record with an archived flag. Leave the flag out of the WHERE clause and every merged deal counts twice. ```sql -- falls in: no archived filter select sum(d.amount*f.dkk_per_unit) from deals d join fx_rates f on f.currency=d.currency  where d.pipeline='new_business' and d.stage in ('lead','qualified','proposal','negotiation') ``` That gives DKK 11,772,774, 7 percent too big. It is the smallest of the three here, and the hardest to spot, because the extra deals have real names and real amounts. ## Trap three: a join that multiplies rows Ask for the open pipeline on deals where the customer's CFO is involved, and the natural query joins deals to contacts and filters on the role. A deal with two CFO rows then appears twice in the join, and its amount is summed twice. ```sql -- falls in: the join fans out select sum(d.amount*f.dkk_per_unit) from deals d join fx_rates f on f.currency=d.currency  join deal_contacts c on c.deal_id=d.id and c.role='CFO'  where d.archived=0 and d.stage in ('lead','qualified','proposal','negotiation') -- steps around it: test for existence instead select sum(d.amount*f.dkk_per_unit) from deals d join fx_rates f on f.currency=d.currency  where d.archived=0 and d.stage in ('lead','qualified','proposal','negotiation')  and exists (select 1 from deal_contacts c where c.deal_id=d.id and c.role='CFO') ``` DKK 6,342,447 against DKK 5,083,337: 25 percent too big. It is the kind of error the Oracle quote on Anthropic's launch page describes, where two unnamed models' SQL overstated totals five to eight times. ![Three horizontal bars on a light ground, one per trap. Each bar runs to a gold line at the true number and carries on in red: CFO rows counted twice gives 25 percent too much, currency not converted 14 percent, merged duplicates counted 7 percent](https://brinvik.com/claude-news/2026-11-09-claude-fable-pipeline/brinvik-pipeline-info-1-faelder-en-1600.jpg) ## Where these traps hide in a real CRM The names differ by system, but the shapes are the same. - **Currency.** Many CRMs store a deal's amount in the deal's own currency and keep a converted amount in the company currency as a separate field. An export may carry one, the other or both. Check which column the agent sums, and with which rate. - **Merged records.** When two records are merged, some systems delete the loser and some keep it with a flag or a pointer to the survivor. An export from the second kind carries both. Look for a column like archived, merged_into or is_deleted before you trust any count. - **Associations.** Deals, contacts and companies are linked many to many. Any question that filters deals by a contact property (role, seniority, country) can multiply rows. The safe pattern is a test for existence, not a join followed by a sum. None of these is exotic. A real CRM export can have all three. ## How the three Claude for business models did against the traps None of them fell into any of the three in 96 runs. All three left out the archived copies in every run and converted currency in every run that asked for kroner. Half the runs had no column documentation at all, only names. Opus and Sonnet answered the same with and without it, and Fable got one more right without it, so the documentation made no difference you could measure here; the models just queried a little more. The models parted ways on two things: one ambiguous question and the price. Sonnet 5.5 and Opus 5.5 matched my answer key 32 times out of 32, Fable 5.2 29 times, and a right answer cost 2.7, 9.3 and 19.9 cents respectively. ## The fourth thing to test: a question with two readings Not a trap in the data, but in the words. "The deals that went into proposal in October" can mean every deal that entered proposal in October (22 deals, DKK 3,563,279) or only those that got there for the first time (17 deals, DKK 2,797,423). 5 deals had been in proposal before and dropped back from negotiation. Fable 5.2 handed in the narrow reading 3 times out of 4, and named both readings and both numbers in its explanation every time. Opus named both once. Sonnet never did; it matched my key because it happened to read the question my way. When you test an agent, include one question like this on purpose and check whether it tells you. ![An animation on a light ground. One deal from the CRM moves across August to November: lead, qualified, proposal in September, negotiation, and back into proposal in October, where October is shaded. Below, two numbers: 22 deals went into proposal in October, 17 got there for the first time](https://brinvik.com/claude-news/2026-11-09-claude-fable-pipeline/brinvik-pipeline-info-3-laesning-en-1080-poster.png) ## A test set you can copy These are the eight questions, in English. Each has one number as its answer, and together they touch every trap above at least once. 1. How big is our open new-business pipeline right now, in Danish kroner? 2. What is our weighted Q4 forecast in kroner? Weight open new-business deals expected to close in the quarter at qualified 20%, proposal 40% and negotiation 70%, and add the new-business deals already won in the quarter at 100%. 3. How much did Mads Krogh win in October, new business and renewals together, in kroner? 4. How much open pipeline do we have on deals where the customer's CFO is involved, in kroner? Both pipelines count. 5. What is the total value in kroner of the deals that went into proposal in October? 6. What was the average size of the new-business deals we won in Q3, in kroner? 7. How many open deals have not changed stage in more than 30 days? 8. How much licence revenue have we won in Q4 so far, in kroner? Only the product Licens. Swap the names, the dates and the product for your own. Work out the true answer yourself first, in SQL and once more some other way, and only then ask the agent. ## How to check an agent's answer - Compare against a number you computed yourself, with a tolerance of about half a percent for rounding. - When an answer is off, check it against the three naive totals. If it matches one, you know which trap it fell into. - Read the explanation, not only the number. Ask for it in the prompt: "First write how you understood the question, and if it can be read more than one way, give the number for each." - Count cost per right answer, not per token. In this test the cheapest model cost 2.7 cents per right answer and the dearest 19.9. Anthropic's own advice is to start with Opus 5.5 and move up or down.
